Nobody could have guessed the challenge of "Raising the Curve" of public schools

A review of the "Raising the Curve" book (author: Ron Berler)

Xie Jiayu Dias
· June 23, 2026 · 3 min read
book-review education

Ron Berler walks us through the routine of each character in a public school throughout the book. He constructs the narrative of the school year's events by weaving together the daily activities of each person — from the well-behaved student to the most troublesome one, from the dedicated librarian to the most devoted literacy specialist, and from the persevering teachers to the unstoppable principal.

Brookside Elementary is a public school from Norwalk, Connecticut. It carries a failing score (along with many other public schools in the same region) and has a chance to change that reputation as it gets prepared to face that year's standardized CMT test.

Although everyone is determined to raise the bar, things do not go as expected. Budget cutbacks affect staffing, forcing Principal Hay to make tough decisions. The children are not motivated to read, and their parents neither help with homework nor attend parent meetings at the school.

It is easy for an outsider to judge the school's results, but the author offers a different perspective by showing the nuances of each person. For instance, he shows how hard it is for a Latino child, whose non-English-speaking parents work long hours, to become a fluent reader when he has neither the resources at home nor the motivation to read. Relying on schoolteachers alone is not sufficient to close that learning gap. By contrast, the small percentage of white children, who have all the resources and support at home, often scores high on the tests. Those that suffer the most come from poorer backgrounds, mostly Black and Hispanic.

Another point of controversy is the CMT test itself. From the perspective of the people that designed it, the goal was to gauge whether students were ready for high school and how public schools compared to private ones. But those preparing the children for the exam felt pressured to produce a score — a score that labels a school as failing or passing, which in turn affects how its administration operates the following year.

Despite the challenges, each student finds a reason to outperform their own previous results. The motivations vary, from the threat of "you are repeating fifth grade" to simply maturing as they grow older.
